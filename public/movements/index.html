    How to Read a Calibre

    What a calibre number means, what separates a workhorse from a landmark, and how to evaluate any movement with five questions.

    A calibre (caliber) is a movement architecture: the layout of bridges, train, escapement, and complications that a manufacturer produces and names. Reading one well is the difference between collecting specifications and understanding machines.

    The five questions

    1. Integrated or modular? Built as a chronograph from the first bridge (El Primero) or a base movement with a module stacked on (many calendar watches)? Integrated wins coherence and thinness; modules win cost and service uniformity.
    2. Who made it, for whom? ETA and Sellita supply the industry; the same base can appear in a $500 watch and a $5,000 one with different finishing. In-house (Rolex’s 3135, Omega’s coaxial family) is a supply-chain and marketing statement as much as a technical one.
    3. What’s the frequency and reserve? 28,800 vph and 40–70h is the modern middle; anything else is a deliberate choice with consequences.
    4. What escapement and spring? Swiss lever with Nivarox is baseline; co-axial + silicon is Omega’s stack; free-sprung balances with Parachrom/Syloxi are Rolex’s. See the balance spring.
    5. How does it service? Parts availability, module access, and whether the design survived long enough for watchmakers to know its habits — the real reason the Valjoux 7750 is everywhere.

    The landmark calibres on this site

    • Omega 321 — the column-wheel chronograph that went to the moon.
    • Zenith El Primero — 5 Hz integrated automatic, 1969.
    • Valjoux 7750 — the cam-lever workhorse of a generation.
    • Rolex 3135 — the argument for boring excellence.
    • Patek Philippe Calibre 89 — what “everything” looked like in 1989.

    Finishing — anglage, perlage, Geneva stripes — shows care but not timekeeping; the numbers above show timekeeping but not care. A great calibre has both, or is honest about which it chose.
