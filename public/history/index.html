    A Timeline of Timekeeping

    From Huygens' pendulum to the Swatch rescue: the chronological archive of how the mechanical watch was invented, perfected, nearly killed, and reborn.

    This is the site’s chronological archive — one entry per turning point, in order. Each entry is a self-contained story; together they are the argument that the mechanical watch is a cultural survivor, not a technical accident.

    The archive

    1. 1656 — Huygens and the Pendulum — clocks learn to swing; timekeeping accuracy jumps 100-fold.
    2. 1675 — The Balance-Spring Race — watches get their oscillator; Hooke and Huygens start a fight.
    3. 1761 — Harrison and the Longitude Prize — a carpenter’s clock beats the astronomers and saves the fleet.
    4. 1770 — Mudge and the Lever — the escapement that wins the future is invented and ignored for decades.
    5. 1850s — The American System — interchangeable parts, machine tools, and the watch becomes an industrial object.
    6. 1927 — The Quartz Clock — the crystal arrives in the laboratory and starts the long countdown.
    7. 1969 — The Quartz Crisis — Seiko’s Astron, the Swiss collapse, and the moment watches stopped being instruments.
    8. 1983 — The Swatch Rescue — Hayek bets on plastic and emotion; mechanical returns as luxury argument.

    How to read it

    Forward for the story of progress; backward for the story of survival. The ending — a market where quartz keeps perfect time for $10 and mechanical watches sell for fortunes — is only contradictory until you read Tourbillon and realize the craft changed what it was selling.
